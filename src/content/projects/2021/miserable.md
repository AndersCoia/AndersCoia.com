## Overview
The goal of this piece was to create a semi‑sarcastic guidebook on how to stay miserable—showing the audience negative habits they might recognise, while also providing a little direction on how to push back against them. The tone is serious on the surface, with humour coming from the overall absurdity of the concept. 

## Concept: A Cult of Misery
The guidebook is framed like a form of religious indoctrination: an explorer’s journal that documents a “garden of misery.” The structure leans into religious imagery—pilgrimage, devotion, forbidden fruit—so the reader feels like they’re being guided through a ritual journey rather than a self-help checklist. 

At the centre are the **six poisons of happiness**, positioned as objects encountered along the path. Each poison has a physical representation drawn from ordinary garden elements—chosen for symbolism and readability. 

## The Six Poisons (Symbol System)
- **Exercise — Bark:** rough at first, but perseverance reveals something smoother underneath.  
- **Diet — Leaf:** a simple shorthand for “healthy,” kept intentionally direct.  
- **Motivation — Flower:** sudden, short, but impactful—like a bloom.  
- **Beyond — Water:** reflections as gateways, but ironically the “gateway” leads outside the self‑imposed enclosure.  
- **Companionship — Root:** family trees and “roots” as relationships that shape who you are.  
- **Rest — Thorn:** “a thorn in my side” — rest reduces misery, making it the natural enemy of the premise.  

## Visual System: Corruption + Progression
An essential element of the information design is the **corruption**—a graphic layer that spreads across the page more aggressively as the poisons become “deadlier.” It breaks monotony across repeated page structures and remains interpretable even without heavy explanation because it matches the theme. 

Illustrations began as raster paintings, then were converted to vector form to keep the texture while gaining scalable, clean output for print and layout refinement. 

## Typography & Colour
The typography system intentionally leans formal and “old text” adjacent—serifs for body and headings, with ornamental initials to reference religious manuscripts. 

The palette is limited and low‑value (paper, ink, deep red, and black) to support an aged‑journal feel and let the corruption read as dominant, invasive contrast. 

## Role
Concept, editorial design, illustration, typography, information design system.
